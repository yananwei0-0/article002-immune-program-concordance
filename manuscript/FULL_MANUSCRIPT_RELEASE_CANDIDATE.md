Abstract
Background
RNA-based scores are often used to describe tumor immune states, although it is unclear how closely they track matched protein and phosphoprotein measurements. We examined eight fixed programs in ten Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) cancer cohorts and used TCGA, APOLLO, single-cell, and spatial data to examine related questions at their respective measurement scales.
Results
The primary analysis included 1,023 cases and 160 registered cancer–program–layer combinations; 129 met the evaluability criteria. All 77 evaluable RNA–protein correlations were positive, and 76 had q < 0.05 (median Spearman ρ = 0.755). All 52 evaluable RNA–phosphoprotein correlations were positive and had q < 0.05 (median ρ = 0.566). Correlation magnitude varied among cancers (descriptive I² proportion, 0.352–0.878). Feature-row weighting preserved the correlation sign in all 129 comparable combinations, and 157 of 158 combinations evaluated under the one-gene recovery threshold had q < 0.05. Thirty-five of 516 finite associations between RNA-adjusted omic scores and immune-context measures had q < 0.05. In TCGA, 4,850 of 5,440 RNA program–immune-metric correlations had q < 0.05; all 17 fixed program–metric pairs were positive in every cancer. In APOLLO, 11 RNA–protein correlations were positive with q < 0.05. None of the five evaluable RNA–phosphoprotein correlations was positive with q < 0.05; the IFN-γ correlation was negative with q < 0.05. Single-cell contrasts varied by source and compartment, and the four breast-cancer spatial samples were analyzed descriptively.
Conclusions
Across the tested CPTAC cancers, RNA scores generally retained patient ordering at the protein level. Phosphoprotein correlations were evaluable for fewer programs and were not reproduced in APOLLO LUAD. RNA scores may approximate relative protein-level program variation in specific cancers and assays, whereas phosphoprotein relationships require separate evaluation.
Keywords: tumor immune ecology; CPTAC; proteogenomics; phosphoproteomics; cross-omic concordance; single-cell RNA sequencing; spatial transcriptomics
Introduction
Tumor immune states arise from interactions among malignant, lymphoid, myeloid, and stromal cells. RNA expression can summarize these states through programs for antigen presentation, interferon signaling, cytolytic activity, checkpoint expression, myeloid inflammation, and TGF-β-associated exclusion. Cytolytic activity varies substantially across tumors [1], and an IFN-γ-related expression profile links chemokine signaling, antigen presentation, cytotoxicity, and adaptive immune resistance [2]. Pan-cancer analyses have also identified recurrent microenvironment classes [3]. Tissue imaging shows that the spatial arrangement of tumor and immune cells adds information beyond their overall abundance [4]. These observations motivate program-level measurements, but the same RNA score can reflect different cellular mixtures and local arrangements.
RNA abundance does not directly measure protein abundance or phosphorylation. Genetic effects may differ between transcripts and proteins [5], and translation and protein turnover can alter their relationship [6]. Matched cancer proteogenomic data have identified pathway activity that was incompletely captured by RNA alone [7]; studies of post-translational modifications similarly show that regulation differs across molecular layers and cancers [8]. Whether an RNA-defined immune program ranks patients similarly at the protein or phosphoprotein level therefore requires direct measurement in matched tumors. Limited feature recovery also matters: some programs may be measurable in RNA but not in an omic layer.
Pan-cancer proteogenomic resources now permit such comparisons. Previous studies have described molecular subtypes and pathways across cancers [9, 10]. Petralia et al. analyzed immune modules in more than 1,000 CPTAC tumors and compared RNA- and protein-based module activity [11]. Here, we applied eight fixed gene programs to matched RNA, protein, and gene-level phosphoprotein data. For each cancer, we report patient-level correlations, retain combinations that fail the coverage rule, and examine the effects of gene recovery and feature weighting. This approach shows both the observed concordance and the combinations that could not be evaluated because of limited coverage.
Bulk-tumor scores cannot establish which cells carry a program. Single-cell studies describe variation in immune composition and state [12] as well as recurring malignant-cell programs and intratumoral heterogeneity [13]. Spatial and single-cell studies of breast and pancreatic cancers show how these populations occupy distinct tissue regions [14, 15]. We therefore measured within-cancer RNA–protein and RNA–phosphoprotein concordance for eight fixed programs across ten CPTAC cohorts, examined sensitivity to scoring choices, and used TCGA RNA, APOLLO proteogenomics, single-cell data, and breast-cancer spatial data to address separate questions about RNA-level alignment, assay transfer, and compartment distribution. These cross-sectional analyses cannot establish clinical prediction or a molecular mechanism.
Methods
Study design
We conducted a cross-sectional, multi-cohort analysis of patient-level concordance among RNA, protein, and phosphoprotein measurements of tumor immune programs. Processed data from ten Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) cancer cohorts formed the primary dataset. The primary estimand was the within-cancer Spearman correlation between matched RNA and protein or phosphoprotein program scores. Cancer-specific correlations were the main results.
Four additional analyses addressed separate questions (Table 1). TCGA RNA data were compared with established immune-expression metrics. APOLLO lung adenocarcinoma and ovarian cancer data were used to examine whether selected RNA–protein relationships appeared in separately processed proteogenomic cohorts. Public single-cell data described program differences among broad cellular compartments within each source, and four breast-cancer Visium samples provided a spatial description. The study included no longitudinal follow-up, clinical outcome, treatment exposure, or experimental perturbation and was not designed to assess prediction or causal mechanisms.
Only processed, de-identified public data were analyzed; no participants or biospecimens were newly recruited or collected for this study.
Data sources and units of analysis
The primary CPTAC analysis included breast cancer (BRCA), clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (CCRCC), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), glioblastoma (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), ovarian cancer (OV), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), and endometrial cancer (UCEC) [16]. Processed RNA, proteome, and phosphoproteome matrices were analyzed together with available ESTIMATE immune and purity scores and CIBERSORT-derived T-cell and myeloid signals [17, 18]. The independent unit was the patient or case.
The TCGA analysis used primary-tumor RNA profiles from the corresponding ten cancer types accessed through UCSC Xena, together with 68 immune metrics and pan-cancer immune-subtype annotations from the TCGA immune-landscape resource [19, 20]. APOLLO analyses used public LUAD RNA, proteome, and phosphoproteome data and public ovarian RNA and proteome data [21, 22]. The single-cell analysis included ten source-specific datasets. Eight were obtained as harmonized H5AD collections from CZ CELLxGENE Discover, and two were obtained through the Curated Cancer Cell Atlas (3CA) [23, 24]. The unit of analysis was a donor with data for paired compartments within a source.
The single-cell datasets were labelled S01–S10 in the supplementary figures and tables. They comprised a multi-tissue tumor-microenvironment atlas (S01) [25], the breast-cancer atlas (S02) [14], two CCRCC datasets (S03 and S04) [26, 27], and COAD, LUAD, HNSCC, GBM, PDAC, and UCEC datasets (S05–S10) [28–33]. Complete collection identifiers and source-to-cancer mappings are provided in the Source_Alias worksheet, Supplementary Table S8b, and the data-provenance record.
The spatial analysis used four breast-cancer Visium samples from the Wu et al. atlas: CID4290, CID44971, CID4465, and CID4535 [14, 34]. The breast-cancer single-cell and spatial data came from the same atlas, so the spatial analysis was treated as a complementary description within that resource. The unit of analysis was the paired sample-level compartment median. Dataset roles, denominators, and units of analysis are summarized in Table 1.
Immune-ecology program definitions
Eight gene programs were fixed for this analysis: MHC class I antigen presentation (16 genes), MHC class II antigen presentation (12 genes), interferon-γ response (21 genes), cytolytic T-cell context (16 genes), checkpoint/exhaustion context (13 genes), myeloid inflammatory context (19 genes), TGF-β/epithelial–mesenchymal transition exclusion context (20 genes), and proteasome antigen processing (22 genes). The first seven represented immune-ecology programs. Proteasome antigen processing was included as a comparison program and was not interpreted as an immune-infiltration signature. Biological scope and representative genes are shown in Table 2, and complete membership, provenance, and CPTAC identifier mappings are provided in Supplementary Table S1.
CPTAC case mapping and program scoring
RNA aliquots annotated as tumor were retained. Unsuffixed RNA identifiers were considered tumor-like only in cohorts in which the processed RNA matrix used unsuffixed identifiers for tumor samples. Adjacent tissue, normal tissue, and quality-control identifiers were excluded. Terminal tumor, adjacent, normal, and technical suffixes were removed to generate normalized case identifiers. If more than one eligible aliquot mapped to a case, an explicitly tumor-labelled aliquot was preferred; remaining ties were resolved deterministically by lexical sample identifier. RNA and each protein layer were then joined one-to-one by normalized case. Uniqueness was required within each cancer–program–layer–case combination.
Feature identifiers were harmonized using trimmed uppercase gene symbols and stable Ensembl identifiers, with Ensembl version suffixes removed. Within each cancer and assay matrix, each retained feature was standardized across eligible tumors using the sample standard deviation. Features with zero or non-finite variation were assigned missing standardized values. Multiple feature rows mapping to the same gene—including multiple phosphosites—were first averaged within sample to obtain one gene-level value. Program scores were then calculated as the unweighted mean of the available standardized gene-level values, thereby giving each recovered gene equal weight. A CPTAC sample score required at least three finite genes from the relevant program. The same rule was applied to RNA, protein, and phosphoprotein scores, and missing program scores were not imputed.
Primary concordance analysis
The primary family contained 10 cancers × 8 programs × 2 comparison layers, yielding 160 registered cancer–program–layer rows. For each row, we calculated the patient-level Spearman correlation (ρ) between matched RNA and protein scores or between matched RNA and phosphoprotein scores. A row was evaluable when it contained at least 20 complete score pairs and at least three distinct values in each score. Rows that failed the gate remained in the complete registry and were reported as not evaluable rather than deleted.
Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were calculated by paired-case bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap with 20,000 resamples [35]. For rows with more than 30 complete pairs and no tied score values, two-sided P values were obtained from the asymptotic Spearman test. Rows with 30 or fewer complete pairs or any ties were evaluated with 999,999 random permutations of the matched omic ranks; the two-sided statistic was the absolute correlation and the plus-one correction was used [36]. Random-number seeds were generated deterministically from SHA-256 hashes of the project, analysis component, cancer, program, and layer identifiers. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied once to the finite P values from evaluable rows within the registered 160-row family [37]. Results with a two-sided q value below 0.05 were considered statistically significant after FDR correction.
Cross-cancer summaries, heterogeneity, and robustness analyses
Cancer-specific correlations remained the primary results. For descriptive comparison across cancers, we reported the median, interquartile range, observed range, number of evaluable cancers, number of positive estimates, and number of estimates with q < 0.05 for each program–layer combination. Heterogeneity was summarized after Fisher z transformation using inverse-variance weights of n − 3. Cochran's Q and I² were calculated using the contributing cancer-specific estimates [38]. I² is reported as a proportion on a 0–1 scale. These statistics described variation across cancers and were not used to define a pooled primary effect.
Three scoring and influence analyses assessed robustness. First, a feature-row sensitivity averaged all standardized feature rows directly, thereby allowing genes represented by multiple phosphosites to receive greater weight. Second, the program-score recovery threshold was lowered from three genes to one gene while retaining the original primary family as the confirmatory analysis. Third, the maximum absolute change in ρ after deleting one matched case was recorded for every evaluable primary row, together with the most influential case identifier. As an additional descriptive analysis, scores were standardized within cancer and concatenated across contributing cancers before calculating a pooled correlation for each program–layer combination. This pooled sensitivity was not interpreted as a pan-cancer inferential estimate.
RNA-adjusted molecular-context analyses
To examine associations between the omic scores and broad immune-context measures after adjustment for the corresponding RNA program score, we fitted an ordinary least-squares regression of each protein or phosphoprotein program score on its matched RNA program score within each evaluable CPTAC stratum. The linear residual was the observed minus fitted omic score. A rank-based sensitivity replaced both scores with average ranks before fitting the same model. Residuals were joined by case to ESTIMATE tumor purity, ESTIMATE immune score, CIBERSORT T-cell signal, and CIBERSORT myeloid signal. Each residual–context association required at least 20 complete pairs and at least three distinct values in both variables. Two-sided Spearman tests were corrected separately for the linear-residual and rank-residual families using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. These analyses were considered secondary descriptions of molecular context; residual association was not interpreted as proof of post-transcriptional regulation or protein-specific mechanism.
TCGA RNA comparison
For each of the ten corresponding TCGA cancer types, RNA-expression samples with primary solid tumor sample-type code 01 were retained from UCSC Xena. The 68 immune metrics and immune-subtype labels were those reported in the TCGA immune-landscape analysis. Samples were sorted by patient and sample identifier, and the lexically first eligible sample was selected when multiple primary-tumor samples were available for one patient. Gene identifiers were harmonized using gene symbols and an Entrez-to-symbol dictionary derived from DepMap Public 26Q1 feature labels [39, 40]. A program was available within a cancer when at least three mapped program genes were represented in the expression matrix. Expression rows were standardized within cancer using the sample standard deviation, and patient-level program scores were the unweighted means of finite standardized rows. Missing values were not imputed.
Within each cancer, the eight RNA program scores were correlated with 68 immune metrics, yielding 5,440 planned tests. A test required at least 25 complete patient pairs and used the two-sided asymptotic Spearman test. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied once across all 5,440 P values. Seventeen fixed program–metric pairs were retained for focused presentation; their complete mapping is provided in Supplementary Table S6c. For each pair, cancer-specific correlations were summarized by their median and the fraction of cancers with a positive coefficient. Because both measures were derived from RNA, this analysis assessed alignment within the transcriptomic layer.
For the separate immune-subtype analysis, program scores were compared across annotated pan-cancer immune subtypes using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Subtype groups with fewer than 20 patients were excluded from inferential ordering but retained descriptively. The eight program-level P values were adjusted as a separate Benjamini–Hochberg family.
APOLLO proteogenomic comparison
For APOLLO LUAD RNA data, sample count tables were combined by an outer union, absent gene–sample combinations were set to zero, and values were transformed as log2(count + 1). Duplicate uppercase gene symbols were averaged. Duplicate gene rows in LUAD proteome, LUAD phosphoproteome, ovarian RNA, and ovarian proteome matrices were likewise collapsed before standardization. Within each matrix, collapsed genes were standardized across samples using the population standard deviation. A program was available when at least three genes were recovered in that layer. Once a program passed this layer-level recovery gate, each sample score was calculated from its available finite standardized genes; an all-missing vector yielded a missing score and no value was imputed.
RNA and omic scores were joined one-to-one by public sample identifier. A cohort–program–layer row required at least 10 matched samples and at least three distinct values in each score. Spearman correlations were accompanied by approximate 95% confidence intervals based on Fisher's z transformation. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was conducted separately within the LUAD protein, LUAD phosphoprotein, and ovarian protein eight-program families. Results were classified as positive with q < 0.05, negative with q < 0.05, nonsignificant, or not evaluable.
Donor-aware single-cell compartment analysis
Single-cell datasets were processed separately by source to avoid treating potentially overlapping public repositories as globally independent. Cells were assigned to malignant, immune, or stromal compartments using source-specific metadata fields, keyword dictionaries, and dataset-specific overrides documented in the analysis mapping rules. Within each source and cancer, counts were aggregated to donor-by-compartment pseudobulk profiles. A donor–compartment group required at least 20 cells, and a program required at least three recovered genes. Pseudobulk counts were converted to counts per million and log1p transformed; duplicate uppercase gene symbols were averaged, and genes were standardized within the source–cancer analytical set using the population standard deviation.
Program scores were calculated as unweighted means of finite standardized genes. Within each donor, we formed malignant-minus-immune, malignant-minus-stromal, and immune-minus-stromal differences. Two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests excluded zero differences (SciPy option zero_method = 'wilcox'), used automatic exact or asymptotic calculation, and applied no continuity correction. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied within each source–cancer family of 24 program-by-contrast tests. The main support rule required at least six paired donors and q < 0.05; a paired-donor threshold of at least ten was examined as a sensitivity analysis. Cross-source summaries were descriptive only because no participant crosswalk established biological donor independence across repositories.
Spatial transcriptomic analysis
Spots from the four breast-cancer Visium samples were assigned to broad malignant, immune, or stromal compartments using the source-provided scores and annotations. Primary assignment used the compartment with the largest positive sum across its source-score columns. When all three sums were non-positive, the supplied classification annotation was used to map stroma to stromal, lymphocyte-containing labels to immune, and invasive-cancer labels to malignant; unresolved spots were excluded.
Within each sample, counts were converted to counts per million and log1p transformed. A program required at least three represented genes. Genes were standardized across eligible spots within sample using the population standard deviation, and each spot-level program score was the unweighted mean of finite standardized genes. For each sample, median scores were calculated by compartment. The three compartment contrasts were then summarized across the four paired sample medians. No spot-level P value was calculated, because spots within a tissue section were not treated as independent patients. The spatial analysis was therefore descriptive.
Software and reproducibility
Analyses were performed in Python 3.14.6 using NumPy 2.5.1, pandas 3.0.5, SciPy 1.18.0, h5py 3.16.0, and Matplotlib 3.11.1. NumPy and SciPy were cited as the principal numerical and statistical computing libraries [41, 42]. Core statistical functions included scipy.stats.spearmanr, scipy.stats.wilcoxon, scipy.stats.kruskal, scipy.stats.rankdata, numpy.linalg.lstsq, and numpy.random.default_rng. Complete evaluable and non-evaluable result registries, model diagnostics, identifier mappings, and the data underlying all figures and tables are supplied as machine-readable supplementary tables.
Results
CPTAC cohort coverage and evaluable comparisons
The ten CPTAC cohorts contributed 1,023 unique cases available in at least one matched RNA–proteome or RNA–phosphoproteome analysis, with cancer-specific maximum matched counts ranging from 82 to 121 (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2). The primary registry contained all 160 registered cancer–program–layer rows. Of these, 129 met the analysis criteria and 31 were not evaluable; every non-evaluable row contained fewer than 20 complete score pairs, with a maximum excluded sample size of 18. The evaluable set comprised 77 RNA–protein and 52 RNA–phosphoprotein rows. No duplicated cancer–program–layer–case key was detected (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S3).
All eight programs were evaluable at the protein level in most cancers, although the cytolytic program was unavailable in one cancer and the checkpoint program in two. At the phosphoprotein level, MHC-I, IFN-γ, TGF/EMT, and proteasome were evaluable in all ten cancers; checkpoint was evaluable in eight and myeloid in four. MHC-II and cytolytic phosphoprotein scores did not meet the complete-pair requirement in any cancer (Table 3).
Widespread within-cancer RNA–protein and RNA–phosphoprotein concordance
RNA–protein concordance was predominantly strong and positive (Figure 1a). Across the 77 evaluable cancer-specific rows, the median ρ was 0.755 and coefficients ranged from 0.012 to 0.950; 76 of 77 had q < 0.05. Program-level median correlations for the seven immune-ecology programs ranged from 0.596 for checkpoint to 0.857 for IFN-γ. IFN-γ correlations had q < 0.05 in all ten cancers and an interquartile range of 0.831–0.893. The proteasome comparison program had a lower median correlation (ρ = 0.329, interquartile range 0.263–0.438). The only primary result with q ≥ 0.05 was PDAC proteasome RNA–protein concordance (n = 105, ρ = 0.012, P = 0.902, q = 0.902).
All 52 evaluable RNA–phosphoprotein correlations were positive (Figure 1b). The median ρ was 0.566, the range was 0.206–0.842, and all 52 had q < 0.05. Among immune-ecology programs with evaluable phosphoprotein data, program-level medians ranged from 0.525 for MHC-I to 0.719 for checkpoint; the IFN-γ median was 0.683. The median for the proteasome comparison program was 0.352. Complete cancer-specific coefficients, BCa confidence intervals, P values, q values, and non-evaluable rows are reported in Supplementary Table S3; program-level summaries are shown in Table 3.
Concordance direction was stable despite between-cancer heterogeneity
All evaluable cancer-specific coefficients were positive, but their magnitudes varied across cohorts (Figure 2a). Descriptive I² proportions ranged from 0.352 to 0.878 across the 14 estimable program–layer combinations (Figure 2b and Table 3). RNA–protein MHC-I had the lowest heterogeneity (I² = 0.352), whereas RNA–protein IFN-γ, cytolytic, and myeloid concordance each had I² above 0.86. At the phosphoprotein level, I² ranged from 0.594 for proteasome to 0.815 for IFN-γ. The direction was consistent across evaluable strata, but the strength of association was not.
Concordance was robust to scoring and case-influence analyses
The primary gene-equal score and the alternative feature-row-weighted score produced the same correlation direction in all 129 jointly evaluable rows (Figure 2c). Protein estimates were identical because recovered protein matrices contributed one feature per mapped gene. Among phosphoprotein rows, the median absolute change in ρ was 0.019. The largest difference occurred for GBM TGF/EMT, for which ρ decreased from 0.791 under gene-equal weighting to 0.526 under feature-row weighting (absolute change = 0.265); the direction remained positive (Supplementary Table S4a).
Lowering the score-recovery threshold from three genes to one increased the number of evaluable rows from 129 to 158, of which 157 had q < 0.05 in the separate sensitivity family (Supplementary Figure S6a and Supplementary Table S4b). The largest leave-one-case change in a primary coefficient was 0.118 for GBM checkpoint RNA–phosphoprotein concordance after exclusion of case C3L-01043. The full-stratum estimate remained positive with q < 0.05 (n = 26, ρ = 0.506, 95% CI 0.062–0.784, q = 0.010; Supplementary Figure S6b and Supplementary Table S3).
Within-cancer-standardized pooled correlations were estimable for 14 of the 16 program–layer combinations. All 14 were positive and ranged from 0.319 to 0.866 (Supplementary Table S4c). MHC-II and cytolytic phosphoprotein combinations remained non-estimable because no cancer met the primary gate. These pooled coefficients were treated only as robustness summaries; the cancer-specific estimates remained primary.
RNA-adjusted residual associations were present in a minority of contexts
The complete residual-context registry contained 640 planned rows. Linear- and rank-residual tests were each finite in 516 rows. Thirty-five associations had q < 0.05 in the linear-residual family and 52 in the rank-residual family; the 35 associations significant in both analyses had concordant directions (Supplementary Figure S2 and Supplementary Table S5). Of the 35 linear-residual associations, 24 involved protein scores and 11 involved phosphoprotein scores. The 35 linear-residual associations were distributed across CIBERSORT myeloid signal (n = 11), ESTIMATE immune score (n = 10), ESTIMATE tumor purity (n = 9), and CIBERSORT T-cell signal (n = 5), with ρ values ranging from −0.452 to 0.541. Residual associations with these four immune-context measures were detected in a minority of evaluable tests; they cannot identify the source of the residual variation. Model diagnostics are reported in Supplementary Table S4d.
TCGA RNA program scores correlated with established immune metrics
The TCGA selection procedure retained 4,780 primary-tumor patients and one selected RNA sample per patient. Because complete immune-metric availability differed from sample selection, the cancer-specific complete-pair denominators used in the correlation registry summed to 4,652; these values ranged from 153 in GBM to 1,082 in BRCA. The resulting 10-cancer × 8-program × 68-metric surface contained all 5,440 planned correlations. After Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the 5,440 tests, 4,850 correlations (89.2%) had q < 0.05 (Supplementary Figure S3 and Supplementary Table S6a).
The 17 fixed program–metric pairs were positive across all ten cancers (Figure 3a). Across the seven immune-ecology programs, median correlations for these fixed pairs ranged from 0.838 for MHC-I to 0.963 for MHC-II; every anchor had a positive coefficient in all ten cancer types. Corresponding median correlations were 0.867 for IFN-γ, 0.900 for cytolytic, 0.891 for checkpoint, 0.878 for myeloid, and 0.923 for TGF/EMT. The proteasome comparator was more weakly aligned with its two anchors (median ρ = 0.337). Both the programs and the comparison metrics were derived from RNA, so these associations assess RNA-level alignment.
Immune-subtype annotations were available for 4,594 of the selected TCGA patients. All eight program scores differed across subtype groups meeting the inferential sample-size gate after FDR correction (Supplementary Figure S4 and Supplementary Table S6d). The immunologically quiet C5 group contained only four patients and was therefore displayed descriptively but excluded from inferential ordering.
APOLLO reproduced several RNA–protein correlations but not the phosphoprotein pattern
APOLLO contributed 87 LUAD sample units with RNA, protein, and phosphoprotein data and 70 ovarian sample units with RNA and protein data. Of 24 registered cohort–program–layer rows, 17 were evaluable: 11 were positive with q < 0.05, five had q ≥ 0.05, and one was negative with q < 0.05; seven rows did not meet program-recovery or matched-sample requirements (Figure 3b and Supplementary Table S7).
Five LUAD protein programs and six ovarian protein programs had positive correlations with q < 0.05. Representative results included LUAD IFN-γ (ρ = 0.894, 95% CI 0.842–0.929, q = 1.42 × 10⁻³⁰) and ovarian IFN-γ (ρ = 0.907, 95% CI 0.853–0.941, q = 2.16 × 10⁻²⁶). None of the five evaluable LUAD phosphoprotein programs had a positive correlation with q < 0.05. Four had q ≥ 0.05, and the IFN-γ correlation was negative (ρ = −0.281, 95% CI −0.464 to −0.075, q = 0.042). Thus, several RNA–protein correlations recurred in APOLLO, whereas the LUAD phosphoprotein results differed from CPTAC.
Single-cell compartment patterns were heterogeneous and source dependent
The public single-cell layer contained 345 source-specific donor units from ten sources. The complete source-level registry contained 336 source–cancer–program–contrast rows, of which 256 met the analysis criteria. Using the paired-donor n ≥ 6 and within-family q < 0.05 criteria, 116 rows were significant: 42 had positive and 74 had negative median paired-donor differences (Figure 4a and Supplementary Figure S5). Significant contrasts included malignant-minus-immune (41 rows), malignant-minus-stromal (31 rows), and immune-minus-stromal contrasts (44 rows), demonstrating that no single compartment relationship characterized every program or source.
Representative GBM source-specific contrasts included myeloid malignant-minus-immune (paired n = 68, median difference = −1.661, q = 5.24 × 10⁻¹²), checkpoint malignant-minus-immune (paired n = 68, median difference = −0.822, q = 5.24 × 10⁻¹²), and proteasome malignant-minus-immune (paired n = 68, median difference = 0.484, q = 5.24 × 10⁻¹²). When the paired-donor threshold was increased to n ≥ 10, 85 rows remained significant, including 31 positive and 54 negative contrasts (Supplementary Tables S8a–S8b). Because donor identity could not be reconciled globally across repositories, these findings describe individual sources but do not establish a pooled cross-source effect or malignant-cell origin.
Spatial contrasts differed across compartments in four breast-cancer samples
All four breast cancer Visium samples yielded compartment-specific program scores. The spatial registry contained 24 program-by-compartment contrasts, each based on four paired sample-level medians (Figure 4b and Supplementary Table S9). Median contrasts ranged from −0.428 to 0.541. Illustrative results included higher MHC-I scores in immune than stromal compartments (median immune-minus-stromal difference = 0.299), lower cytolytic scores in malignant than immune compartments (median malignant-minus-immune difference = −0.348), and higher proteasome scores in malignant than stromal compartments (median difference = 0.541). With only four samples, these contrasts were descriptive; no spot-level or patient-level significance test was performed.
Discussion
In ten CPTAC cancers, RNA and protein scores for tumor immune programs generally ranked matched patients similarly: all 77 evaluable correlations were positive, 76 had q < 0.05, and the median ρ was 0.755. All 52 evaluable RNA–phosphoprotein correlations were also positive with q < 0.05, with a median ρ of 0.566; several programs lacked sufficient phosphoprotein coverage. Correlation size differed across cancers even when direction did not. Alternative scoring and leave-one-case analyses did not change the direction of the primary correlations. APOLLO reproduced several RNA–protein correlations but not the CPTAC phosphoprotein pattern. TCGA showed RNA-level alignment, while the single-cell and spatial analyses described variation across cellular compartments.
The RNA–protein correlations do not imply that the two assays measure identical states. Tissue and cancer-cell-line atlases show that protein levels reflect RNA abundance together with translational and turnover processes [43, 44]. Immune-cell content can also vary markedly between bulk tumors and may raise both RNA and protein program scores in the same patients. We did not estimate how much of the observed concordance reflects cell composition versus expression changes within cells. Averaging genes into a program may stabilize patient ordering, but we did not test that explanation against single-gene scores. Petralia et al. had previously compared RNA- and protein-based immune module activity [11]. The present analysis focuses on fixed programs, cancer-specific effect sizes, phosphoprotein coverage, and scoring sensitivity.
Phosphoprotein scores warrant a narrower interpretation. MHC-II and cytolytic scores failed the primary coverage rule in every cancer. The overall median RNA–phosphoprotein correlation was lower than the RNA–protein median, but the two summaries drew on different sets of evaluable strata; they are not a paired test of the assay layers. Phosphorylation varies by site and regulatory context, while assay coverage varies among proteins and cohorts [8, 45]. We averaged detected phosphosites to genes before scoring, so these scores cannot estimate site occupancy or kinase activity. APOLLO LUAD showed no positively supported phosphoprotein correlation among its five evaluable programs and an opposite-direction IFN-γ result. Differences in samples, preprocessing, and phosphosite recovery could contribute to this discrepancy, but the current data cannot distinguish their contributions.
The shared positive direction did not imply a common effect size. Descriptive I² estimates showed substantial variation across cancers, consistent with differences in tumor purity, cellular composition, stromal organization, and immune phenotype documented in pan-cancer studies [46, 47]. The weaker proteasome results are also informative: gene aggregation and standardization did not yield strong concordance for every program. Our data cannot identify which biological or technical factors produced the stronger correlations for individual immune programs.
After adjustment for the corresponding RNA score, 35 of 516 finite linear-residual associations with four immune-context measures passed FDR correction. Relatively few associations with those measured contexts therefore remained detectable. Residual correlations cannot tell whether the surviving relationships reflect cell mixture, protein regulation, measurement differences, or other factors. The four context measures also capture partly overlapping features of immune infiltration, which varies among cancer types [48]. These analyses do not quantify the fraction of protein variation explained by RNA or establish an RNA-independent immune mechanism.
TCGA and APOLLO addressed different questions. TCGA showed that the fixed RNA programs aligned with established immune-expression metrics in the corresponding cancers, including positive correlations for every canonical anchor. Because both sides of those comparisons were based on RNA, shared genes or correlated cell abundance could strengthen the associations. APOLLO tested RNA against protein in separately processed LUAD and ovarian resources, where several positive relationships recurred [49, 50]. It covered only two cancer types and did not reproduce positive phosphoprotein concordance. Its results indicate transfer for selected protein-level relationships in these settings, without establishing independence of donors or generalization to every CPTAC cancer.
Single-cell contrasts differed by program, compartment, and source, consistent with reports of recurring cellular programs alongside variable tumor microenvironments [51, 52]. A bulk score may change because cell proportions change, expression within cells changes, or both. Source-specific pseudobulk comparisons used donors as the paired unit, but donor overlap between repositories and differences in compartment annotation remain unresolved. The four breast-cancer spatial samples showed compartmental differences consistent with previously described tissue organization [53, 54]. Their spot labels were informed by expression-derived source scores, so the RNA program contrasts are not independent of the compartment assignment. Together with the four-sample size, this limits the spatial result to an illustration of within-atlas patterns.
The analysis retained all 160 registered cancer–program–layer combinations, including the 31 that failed the coverage criteria. Comparisons used matched cases within cancer, with a single FDR adjustment across the 129 finite primary tests. Feature weighting, a lower gene-recovery threshold, and leave-one-case analyses examined sensitivity to scoring and influential observations. The supporting datasets were analyzed at their own patient, donor, or sample level.
The analysis also depends on processed public data generated with different sampling and normalization procedures. Fixed, equally weighted programs may miss cancer-specific genes, opposing gene effects, isoforms, or differences in measurement reliability. Phosphosite coverage was incomplete, and collapsing sites to genes obscured site-specific regulation. TCGA comparisons were RNA only; APOLLO covered two cancers and was not confirmed to be donor independent of other resources. Single-cell compartments relied on source metadata, and participants could overlap across collections. Spatial results came from four samples in one breast-cancer atlas. No clinical outcomes, treatment exposures, longitudinal observations, or perturbation experiments were examined, so the molecular correlations do not establish clinical prediction or causation.
RNA program scores may be useful as approximations of relative protein-level variation for programs that show consistent within-cancer concordance. Their performance should be checked in the cancer and assay of interest, with separate validation for phosphoprotein scores. Matched cohorts generated under consistent protocols, with site-resolved phosphoproteomics and donor-linked cellular or spatial data, would help determine which relationships are reproducible. Clinical data would then be needed to test whether any of these scores predict outcomes or treatment response.
Conclusions
Fixed tumor immune programs showed positive RNA–protein concordance in all 77 evaluable CPTAC cancer strata, with FDR support in 76. RNA–phosphoprotein correlations were positive in evaluable CPTAC strata but had lower median magnitude and incomplete program coverage; the positive phosphoprotein pattern did not recur in APOLLO LUAD. The results indicate that RNA scores can approximate relative protein-level variation for some programs and cancers. Their performance should be confirmed in the intended assay and cohort before clinical use.
